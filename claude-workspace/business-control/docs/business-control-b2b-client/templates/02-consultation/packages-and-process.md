## What you're actually buying

Not a website. A system your business runs on, with a shop attached.

**The front** — a storefront customers buy from, installable on a phone like
an app without going near an app store, fast on a bad connection, and
editable by you without calling anyone.

**The back** — one screen for orders, stock, staff, suppliers, routes and
numbers, where each person sees their own job and nothing else.

**The joins** — the tools you already pay for, wired in, so the same fact is
never typed twice.

You own all of it: the code, the data, the accounts, the domain. There is no
platform that can raise your rent or switch you off.

## The five phases

Each phase ends at a gate. Nothing proceeds until you sign off, and nothing
new gets added after sign-off without a written change order — that clause
protects your budget more than it protects our schedule.

### 1 · Discover — week 1

Goals, audience and constraints. A content audit of what exists and what has
to be written. Access to the systems we'll connect. It ends in a requirements
document: every page, every screen, every role, every integration, in plain
language.

**Gate:** you sign the requirements. This is the thing we both point at for
the rest of the project. It is worth reading slowly.

### 2 · Design — weeks 2–4

Your brand applied to the storefront, then the direction for everything else.
You see the homepage first, because it settles the argument about look and
feel before it can spread across forty screens.

**Gate:** feedback round 1 — one consolidated response within five working
days.

### 3 · Build — weeks [4–10]

Storefront and checkout, then the back office, then your content and data
moved in. You get a working link from early on and it stays working — you
watch it fill up rather than waiting for a reveal.

**Gate:** feedback round 2, on the full system.

### 4 · Launch — weeks [11–12]

The launch checklist: redirects so you keep your search rankings, analytics,
payments tested with real money, backups, performance and accessibility
passes. Then a recorded training session and the handover pack.

**Gate:** you own the keys — every account in your name, every password
yours, the source code yours outright.

### 5 · Grow — ongoing

A care plan if you want one: hosting, backups, updates, monitoring and a
block of change hours each month. Cancel any time. New features are quoted
as they come up.

## The timeline, honestly

| Phase | From scratch | If a prototype already exists |
|---|---|---|
| Discover | 1 week | 1 week |
| Design | 3 weeks | [1–2] weeks |
| Build | [6] weeks | [4] weeks |
| Launch | 2 weeks | 2 weeks |
| **Total** | **[12] weeks** | **[8] weeks** |

**Two things move these dates, and only two.**

**Content.** Every project that has ever run late ran late because copy and
photography arrived late. It is the critical path from day one. If writing
isn't going to happen in-house, add copywriting to the proposal now — it is
cheaper than the delay it prevents.

**Decisions.** One decision-maker, roughly two hours a week. Feedback from a
committee, arriving in pieces, is the other way timelines die.

Everything else — the building — is our problem, and we've priced for it
going wrong occasionally.

## Money

| Project value | Schedule |
|---|---|
| Under $5k | 100% up front, or 50/50 |
| $5k – $15k | 50% deposit, 50% before launch |
| $15k – $40k | 40% deposit, 30% at design sign-off, 30% before launch |
| Over $40k | 30% deposit, then monthly against milestones |

The deposit books your slot in the schedule. Nothing starts without a
signature and a cleared deposit — that protects both of us, and it is not
negotiable in either direction.

Two feedback rounds are included and written into the contract. A third is a
paid add-on rather than a favour, because "one more small round" is how
projects quietly become unprofitable and then resentful. Anything not in the
signed requirements is a change order: a short written note, a price, and your
yes before any work happens.
