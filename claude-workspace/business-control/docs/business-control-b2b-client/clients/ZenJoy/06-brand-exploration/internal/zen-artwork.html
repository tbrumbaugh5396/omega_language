Watch the two risks: zen tips into wellness cliché (bamboo, pebbles, spa beige) or into sedation (sleepy, inert). The studies below lean on clearance, balance and flow — awake calm — and avoid the spa shelf.

Ten studies

The narrative — one motif, whole page

The studies above are vocabulary. This is the sentence: noise → the drop → the eclipse → the landing → the ripple → stillness. One water line runs unbroken from the top of the page to the footer, choppy at the start and flat by the end. The visitor never sees a "section" — they see the sea calming as they scroll, which is the product claim, made structurally instead of in copy.

BEAT 1 · HERO The noise

Choppy water, almost black. No product yet. Emotion: recognition — "that's my Tuesday." Copy sits in the trough: "Some days come at you in waves."

BEAT 2 · SCROLL 1 The drop rises

A single warm droplet launches out of the chop — the first orange on the page. Emotion: anticipation. This is the product entering, and it enters from the problem, not beside it.

BEAT 3 · PRODUCT The eclipse

The drop meets the sun and holds — for one scroll-length they're the same object. Emotion: alignment. The hero product shot lives exactly here; the drop is the can, seen end-on.

BEAT 4 · BENEFIT The landing

Impact. A crown of spray, then quiet. Emotion: release — the exhale. One line of copy, nothing else on screen: "…and then it doesn't."

BEAT 5 · FLAVOURS The ripple

Rings spread outward and the water flattens behind them. Each ring is a flavour. Emotion: choice, play. Cans stand in the calm they made — the shelf shot earns its place because the sea is already still.

BEAT 6 · CTA Stillness

Glass water, one horizon, vast empty space — study 08 (Ma) as a full section. Emotion: calm ownership. The only thing in it is the button. Space makes it unmissable.

The emotional arc

The five rules that keep it one thing

1 · The water line never breaksOne continuous horizon from hero to footer, choppy → glass. Sections butt against it; nothing has its own frame.
2 · Orange appears once, then earns itNo warm colour until the drop. After the landing, orange is only ever the CTA. Scarcity makes it a signal.
3 · Backgrounds lighten as you scrollNear-black to paper. The visitor feels the release before reading a claim about it.
4 · Every ring is a flavourConcentric geometry is the brand grammar — cans, badges, loaders, the cursor. Change only hue.
5 · Space grows toward the endPadding widens beat to beat. The page literally opens up, so the CTA sits in the most air on the site.

Cheapest way to prove it: the drop's flight is a single SVG path. Tie the droplet's position along that path to scroll offset and the whole story animates with about fifteen lines of JS — no video, no library, and it degrades to a perfectly good static illustration when motion is reduced.

Resolving "some days come at you in waves"

The payoff to protect is "…and then it doesn't." It works because it under-plays — no claim, no adjective, just the weather changing. Everything below is built to make that line land harder, not to replace it.

One craft note first — the antecedent. "Some days come at you in waves" is plural ("days… come"); "and then it doesn't" is singular. Spoken fast, nobody objects — "it" reads as the whole situation. But on a quiet page with three seconds of silence around it, the mismatch is faintly felt. Three clean fixes, all keeping your line intact: make the opener singular ("The day comes at you in waves"), let the water be the antecedent (put a line about the water between them), or keep the plural opener and let "it" mean the coming-at-you — which is defensible, and the deadpan is worth the grammatical shrug. Sets below show each.

Tea changes the motif — and improves it

The big idea: for a canned soda the ripple was a metaphor. For tea it's literally what happens in the cup — something enters still water and colour spreads outward in rings until the whole vessel is changed. The steep is the story. That means the hero film needs no actors, no VFX and no ocean: a macro shot of an infusion blooming, shot once, is the entire brand arc in eight seconds.
⏳Three minutes is the product Steeping "wastes" 3 minutes — that's not a drawback, it's the entire benefit. You're not selling leaves, you're selling a sanctioned pause. Own the timer: on pack, on site, as a QR that starts a 3-minute breathing screen. STRONGEST IDEA
🔇The audio signature changes A can cracks; tea doesn't. The signature becomes the kettle click → pour → first silence. Quieter and more ownable — and it works in a library, which is where half your customers are. REVISION
🌡Two temperatures, one motif Hot: steam rings rising. Iced: condensation rings on the glass. Same geometry, two occasions — morning steadying and afternoon reset — without a second visual language. RANGE
🍃Ingredients as characters L-theanine the quiet one, ashwagandha the steady one, lemon balm the kind one, chamomile the old friend. Each gets a ring colour and one sentence — botany with a personality, not a supplement panel. FORMULATION
🍵Ceremony you've actually earned Every other brand borrows "zen." Tea has a real 400-year ceremony behind it. You can reference chadō honestly — the ritual is native to the category, not appropriated for a soda. AUTHENTICITY
📦Two formats, one arc Sachets for the desk and the 3-minute ritual; RTD cans of the same formula, iced, for the commute. The film's arc covers both — the drop lands in a cup or a can. ARCHITECTURE
🧭Don't sell calm — sell composure Every tea on the shelf owns "calm"; it's table stakes and it sounds like sleep. Composure is calm under load — it implies a demanding life, which is flattering, and it's exactly what the waves motif already says. DIFFERENTIATION
🌀The leaf is the drop Recast the golden drop as the sachet descending, or a single leaf turning as it falls. Keeps the entire storyboard intact and makes the hero object the thing you actually sell. MOTIF FIX
🎞Shoot once, cut forever One macro rig, one afternoon: a dozen infusion blooms in different colours. That's the hero film, every 6-second cut, all flavour headers and the loading animation — from a single shoot day. PRODUCTION
🫖The pour as the eclipse Water arcing from kettle to cup is the drop's flight path. Shot backlit, the stream catches light exactly like the sun-eclipse frame — same beat, real footage, no compositing. CINEMATOGRAPHY
Claims guardrail — read before writing a word of pack copy. A stress-relief formulation puts you in structure/function territory, and the line is sharper than most founders expect. Safer: "supports a calm mind," "helps you feel steady," "traditionally used to unwind," and letting the ingredient carry the story rather than an outcome promise. Riskier: "relieves anxiety," "treats stress," "reduces cortisol" — those describe treating a condition and invite regulatory attention plus a substantiation burden. Practical rule for this brand: describe the moment, not the mechanism. "Three minutes that are yours" is unimpeachable and does more selling than any biochemistry — and conveniently it's the same discipline that keeps the copy good. Get a regulatory read before print regardless; this is a flag, not advice.

Canned — what that changes

Correcting the previous slide honestly. Some of it was written for loose leaf and doesn't survive contact with a can. Dies: "three minutes is the product." Nobody steeps a can — the single best idea on that slide is gone as written, and pretending otherwise would cost you a shoot. Comes back: the crack. The audio signature returns to the can opening — and it's better than the kettle, because the crack is the drop landing. One sound, one story beat. Survives untouched: the waves motif, "composure not calm," the ripple-as-flavour system, and the whole storyboard — the drop simply lands in a can instead of a cup.

Sparkling — decided, and what it unlocks

Three things resolve the moment you commit to sparkling. ① The sound design is finished: crack → hiss → pour → silence. Four beats, no music needed, and the hiss is the one sound a still tea can never own. ② The sweet-tea reflex is broken for free: carbonation reads "refreshment," not "gallon of sweet tea." The format argues your positioning before a word is read. ③ You gain a second motif layer: bubbles are ripples that travel up. Rings spread out, bubbles rise — same geometry, two directions, and the can is full of them.

Connecting the two lines

Your inclusivity note is the most commercially important call here. If the brand only makes sense on a bad day, you've capped frequency and made buying it a small admission of struggle. Two fixes, and they're the same edit: drop "at you" (which is adversarial) and move from "some days" to every day. Waves were never only bad — a great day comes in waves too: rush, momentum, too much at once. A wave is motion, not misfortune. Bonus: "Every day comes" is singular, which quietly fixes the antecedent problem — "and then it doesn't" now agrees.
The rhyme, precisely. Days /deɪz/ and waves /weɪvz/ share the vowel and the final z — everything but the v. That's a slant rhyme so close it lands like a rhyme without the sing-song a perfect one brings. It's why the line feels good and doesn't feel like an ad. The untapped move: days and ways is a true rhyme — so a third beat can close what the first two open.

If you only pick two

Pair one mark with one world. Ensō (01) or Ripple (07) works as the mark — ownable, debossable, animatable, legible at 12px on a bottle cap. Overprint (06) or Cyanotype (10) works as the world — the texture behind photography, on cartons and in the feed. Ma (08) isn't artwork so much as a rule: whatever you choose, leave more room around it than feels comfortable. That single discipline is what will read as "zen" long after the motif changes.
