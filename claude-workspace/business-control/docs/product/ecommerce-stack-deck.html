The money-model overlay — for-profit ⇄ nonprofit, not a vertical

Flipping it swaps reporting (P&L ⇄ program/fund accounting + tax receipts), governance roles (shareholder · investor ⇄ director · board · donor), and pricing (nonprofit −30%). The mechanics barely change: recurring giving is the subscription engine, volunteers are unpaid event staff — which is why the overlay is cheap to support.

Boxes decide what · tier decides how much (quotas, locations, support) · money model decides who governs — same core, same quote builder, same exit-is-a-file promise.

Honest scope: Learning v1 ships lessons · quizzes · derived progress (the classroom phase — live sessions, attendance, teacher payroll — is the next port); nonprofits add a thin compliance layer (receipts + grant exports); and every combination we allow is a combination we test — bundles bound the QA surface. Roles & workflows · the money side

Treasury — when the balance sheet becomes an operating surface

A business that clears real revenue stops being only a P&L and starts being a balance sheet. Cash sits somewhere. Reserves earn — or don't. Input costs move with markets we don't control. Past a certain size that stops being the founder's spare-evening spreadsheet and becomes three roles with real workflows.

💹 Treasurer / money manager Scale tier

Owns cash and the asset register. Workflow: daily cash position across accounts → 13-week rolling forecast → runway against burn → allocation policy (operating float / reserve ladder / long-term) → interest & yield income booked back to the ledger. Alerts when the operating float drops below n weeks of payroll + payables.

📉 Portfolio trader Scale tier

Owns positions. Workflow: position book with cost basis → realised vs unrealised P&L → exposure limits by asset class and single-name concentration → a trade journal (thesis, entry, exit, what actually happened) → period attribution. Rebalance proposals get raised here and approved by the treasurer — two people, never one.

🌍 Macro analyst Scale tier

Owns the outside world. Workflow: rates, FX and commodity curves in → mapped onto the bill of materials we already store → scenario models ("aluminium +18%, diesel +$0.40, EUR −6%: what happens to gross margin and to the route P&L") → hedging and forward-buy recommendations to purchasing.

Why this belongs in the ERP and not in a separate app

The macro role is the giveaway. For a beverage brand, “macro exposure” isn't an abstraction — it's aluminium, tea, sugar, freight, diesel and FX, and every one of those is already a line in the BOM, the purchase orders and the route costs we hold. A standalone treasury tool has to be told what the business buys. Ours already knows.

Existing data we already holdWhat treasury does with it

Scope line — we record, we don't execute

Broker and bank connections are read-only. We hold no custody, route no orders, and give no investment advice — the moment a product does any of those it is a regulated entity (broker-dealer / RIA / money transmitter, depending), and that is a different company with a different licence. Execution stays at the customer's broker; we are the system of record and analysis sitting over it. This line is a feature, not a limitation: it's what lets us ship treasury as ordinary software.

Why it's a tier driver

Treasury only matters once there's money to manage — which makes it a natural Scale-tier unlock, sold to the exact customers who have outgrown Starter pricing. It also brings the CFO into the account: the finance seat is the hardest one to displace and the least likely to churn. Depends on Finance, which depends on Accounting — so it drags two boxes with it.

Same rule as every other role: adopt ours or wire in yours. A company already running a treasury workstation keeps it and we read positions in; a company running it out of a spreadsheet finally stops.

Roles & workflows · the technical side

API & data platform — the admin views a developer actually needs

Every deal above ~20 seats has a technical buyer in the room, and they ask the same three questions: can I get my data out, can I wire this to what we already run, and can I test it without breaking production? A "no" on any of them loses the deal regardless of how good the ops features are. This is the box that answers all three.

🔌 API / integrations engineer Pro tier

Workflow: mint a scoped key → read the OpenAPI spec → build against the API explorer → register webhook endpoints → watch the delivery log → replay what failed. Keys are scoped per capability, stored hashed, shown once.

🗄 Data engineer / DBA Scale tier

Workflow: browse the schema → run saved read-only queries → schedule exports to their warehouse → review migrations before they apply → verify a restore, not just a backup. Untested backups are the ones that fail.

🧪 QA / release engineer Scale tier

Workflow: issue test-mode keys → spin a seeded sandbox tenant → run contract tests against the OpenAPI spec in their own CI → run integrity checks → promote. Nobody should ever be testing against live orders.

The data console is read-only and permissioned like every other admin surface — it runs through the same check() choke point and writes to the same audit log. A free-text SQL box that can write to a customer's production database is a breach waiting for a date; a read-only, logged, saved-query surface is a feature. Exports are scoped to the tenant, never across tenants.

Why it closes deals

"Can I get my data out?" is a lock-in question, and the honest answer — full API, OpenAPI spec, scheduled exports — removes the objection instead of arguing with it. The same openness that makes us easy to leave is what makes us easy to buy, and it's the ground the app platform is built on: partners can only extend what they can reach.

The biggest gap on the roadmap

Automation — the difference between a record system and an operating system

Every box in this deck records what happened. None of them act on it. A workflow engine is the single missing primitive that turns thirty capability boxes into one system that runs the business while nobody is looking — and it is the biggest hole in the roadmap as it currently stands.

Why we can build it cheaply

The hard part of a workflow engine is usually the integrations. We already have them: every action above is an endpoint that already exists and is already tested. The engine is a trigger table, a condition evaluator and a job runner over the public API — small, and it makes thirty existing boxes worth more without touching any of them.

The competitive angle

Shopify Flow is the closest analogue and it's Plus-only — and it can only see the storefront. It cannot trigger on a missed shift, a late route, a supplier lead time or a cash-floor breach, because Shopify doesn't hold those. Automation across storefront + operations in one engine is the thing our architecture can do and theirs structurally cannot.

Why it drives tiers

Rule count and run volume are clean, honest meters — Starter gets a handful of prebuilt rules, Pro authors its own, Scale gets approval chains and unlimited runs. It's also the stickiest feature in the product: a business with forty rules encoding how it actually operates does not migrate. Automation is the retention moat.

Status: ⧖ not built — new Automation box in MANAGEMENT, next to Intelligence. The pairing is deliberate: Intelligence sees the business, Automation acts on it. Prebuilt rule packs per bundle (food brand, course, nonprofit, food bank) would ship as templates — which makes them portfolio and services artefacts too.

The engine only works if every subsystem is reachable and replaceable — that architecture is the next slide, and the exact contracts to build against are in the extension contracts ↓.

Architecture · build reference

Everything is a slot — one rule makes the whole stack swappable

The rule, stated once: no code calls a vendor directly. Every external capability is reached through providers.get("slot"), and every slot ships an http implementation that forwards a normalised payload to a URL you configure. Swapping a subsystem is then a config row, not a refactor — and anything that speaks HTTP+JSON can take over any part of the system.

Why this is worth doing before scale, not after

Retrofitting slots into a codebase that calls vendors inline is one of the genuinely expensive refactors, and it gets more expensive every month. Doing it early is cheap here because the surface is still small — and the catalog adapter already proves the pattern works: the storefront was built against it from day one and has never touched an ERP table directly.

What it unlocks commercially

"We already use Avalara / Twilio / Okta / Shopify" stops being an objection and becomes a config row. It also makes the hybrid path real — a customer can keep Shopify as the catalog and buy the ERP, or keep their ERP and buy the storefront. Half of the addressable market is already running something; slots are how we sell to them without a rip-and-replace.

Scaling the product — who's buying

Organizations: one buyer, many tenants

A tenant is one business's isolated world — one SQLite file, all its people and locations inside it. What was missing is the layer above: the account that pays. Add organizations now and the reseller motion is a config change later; bolt it on after launch and it's a migration.

① Direct — one business

The default and the primary motion. One org, one tenant, locations and staff inside it. Most customers never know the org layer exists.

② Multi-brand parent

Separate brands or legal entities that must not share data — but one payer, one bill, one roll-up. Isolation is the feature, not a limitation.

③ Reseller / franchisor

An agency, franchisor or distributor owning dozens of tenants — the distribution channel. Volume pricing per tenant, org staff who can cross in to support, and each franchisee still fully isolated.

Your own food business already speaks this language: a distributor with many accounts is motion ③. The roles board even has the vocabulary — this is the same shape, one level up.

The go-to-market

Services are the acquisition channel — not the business

Software runs at ~96% gross margin and scales without headcount. Services run at 30–50% and scale with people. Blur them and the first question in any room becomes “is this a product company or an agency?” — so we don't put services in as a revenue line. We put them in as the cheapest customer acquisition we have.

The rule that prevents the agency trap

No custom work ships unless it produces a reusable section, preset or template that lands in the product within one release. Do it once for a client; every tenant then pays monthly for it. Services become a feature factory — the work compounds instead of evaporating. the loop, drawn ↓

Never sell services alone

Every engagement includes the subscription. A services-only deal is the transaction that quietly turns a software company into an agency — and it's the one deal we decline. Price services slightly uncomfortably so the product stays the easy path.

The mix is a trajectory, not a state

Services ≈60% of revenue in year one, ≈25% by year three — stated up front, because naming the curve yourself is what protects the software multiple. Services are the wedge; ARR is the business.

And the portfolio builds itself: the four bundles ship as live reference storefronts — food brand, course business, language nonprofit, food bank — which are simultaneously the sales demo, the template library new tenants clone from, and the proof that the design capability is real. Zenjoy is reference storefront #1, and it already exists.

The margin curve

Build #1 reuses ~80% of the platform. By build #10 the template library, section types and presets push that past 90% — same price to the client, materially less delivery cost to us. Services margin improves with volume, which is not normally true of services.

The library is the moat

Every engagement adds sections, presets and reference storefronts. A competitor can copy the software; they can't copy 200 production-tested templates built from real brands' real problems.

It kills services over time

As the library grows, more customers self-serve — which is exactly the 60%→25% services curve on the previous slide. The goal of the services business is to make itself unnecessary, and the loop is how that happens on purpose.

Enforcement is a single line in the definition of done: a build isn't shippable until its reusable artefact is merged. No exceptions, because the exceptions are how agencies happen.

The portfolio — top of the services funnel

Reference · how we ship

Test & release automation — what one process serving many tenants needs

Single-tenant bugs embarrass you. Multi-tenant bugs end you. Going from "runs one brand" to "runs four hundred" is mostly a testing and release-safety problem, not a feature problem — so it belongs on the roadmap as explicitly as any feature does.

The roadmap, ordered by what actually bites first
ToolingStatusWhy it's on the list
Tenant isolation suite⧖ automated cross-tenant access attempts on every endpoint. Tenant A seeing tenant B's orders is the one bug that ends the company — it must be a test, not a code review
CI on every push⧖ the 149 checks exist and pass; nothing forces them to run before a deploy. Cheapest item here by a wide margin
Load test that validates the capacity model⧖ the whole cluster cost model rests on 25 units/node — a number we asserted and have never measured. If it's really 12, the margin slides in this deck are wrong
Usage metering reconciliation⧖ seats, locations, SMS, storage are billed from counters. A metering bug is a revenue bug and is invisible until a customer disputes an invoice
Synthetic checkout canary⧖ a robot places a real test order every few minutes. Finding out checkout is broken from a customer email is the worst possible monitoring
Feature flags + cohort rollout⧖ ship to 5 tenants, then 50, then all — with a kill switch. Without flags, every release is all-or-nothing across the whole book
Migration up/down + restore drill⧖ a schema change that half-applies across many tenant DBs is a very bad weekend. An untested backup is not a backup
Error tracking + per-tenant tracing⧖ "it's slow" needs to resolve to a tenant, an endpoint and a query — support cost per tenant is what decides whether the low tiers are profitable
Visual regression on sections/themes⧖ customers compose their own pages from our sections. A CSS change can silently break a live storefront we've never seen
Accessibility (WCAG) checks⧖ ecommerce accessibility suits are common and land on the merchant. Automated checks in the theme editor are a real selling point, not just hygiene
Dependency + secret scanning⧖ table stakes the moment a customer's security questionnaire arrives — and they arrive at exactly the deal sizes we want
End-to-end suite (149 checks)✓ already covers storefront, checkout, discounts, refunds, webhooks, permissions — one command
The honest one to say out loud

The capacity number. Every infrastructure cost, margin and tier-price slide downstream of the cluster model inherits 25 units/node, and that figure is an engineering estimate rather than a measurement. It's cheap to settle — a day of load testing — and until it's settled the right way to present those slides is "modelled", not "measured". Better we flag it than an investor's technical diligence does.

The order is the argument

Isolation and CI first because they protect against unrecoverable failures. Load and metering next because they protect the numbers in this deck. Canary, flags and tracing after, because they protect the operating margin as tenant count grows. Visual, accessibility and scanning last — real, but they cost deals rather than companies.

Architecture · build reference

The four contracts — everything extensible reduces to these

Four JSON shapes. Every extension point in the system is one of them, which is the whole design: a small number of contracts, each reachable over HTTP, each storable as a row. Nothing here requires a plugin to run our code — which is what keeps a plugin from taking the platform down.

① Event envelope in and out

Every meaningful thing that happens becomes one of these. Emitted internally, delivered to webhooks, and accepted inbound at POST /api/v1/events so an outside system can drive our rules.

{
  "event": "inventory.below_par",
  "id":    "evt_9f3c…",      // idempotency key
  "ts":    1776453008.42,
  "tenant":"acme",
  "source":"core",           // core | api | ext:<name>
  "data":  { "sku": "ZJ-12", "on_hand": 4,
             "par": 24, "location": "wh-2" }
}

Naming: noun.verb_past, always. order.created, payment.failed, shift.missed. The registry of event names is the API — treat renames as breaking changes.

② Rule declarative automation

Data, never code. A rule is a row; the evaluator is a small interpreter over these fields. No eval, ever — conditions are structured or they are a remote-code-execution hole.

{
  "when": "inventory.below_par",
  "if": [
    {"field":"data.location","op":"eq","value":"wh-2"},
    {"field":"data.on_hand", "op":"lt","value":5}
  ],
  "then": [
    {"action":"purchase_order.create",
     "with":{"supplier":"auto","qty":"{{par - on_hand}}"}},
    {"action":"http.post",
     "with":{"url":"https://acme.example/restock"}}
  ],
  "approval": {"required":true,"role":"purchasing"},
  "enabled": true, "rate_limit": "10/hour"
}
③ Provider binding swap a subsystem

One row per slot. The http impl is the universal escape hatch — it makes every slot swappable for anything that speaks JSON, with no code on our side.

{
  "slot": "rates",
  "impl": "http",          // builtin | http | ext:<name>
  "config": {
    "url":     "https://acme.example/rates",
    "secret":  "env:ACME_RATES_KEY",  // by reference only
    "timeout_ms": 2500,
    "on_error": "fallback_builtin"   // or "fail"
  }
}

Non-negotiable: secrets stored by reference and never echoed back by any read endpoint; every slot declares a timeout and an on_error policy, so a slow vendor degrades instead of taking checkout down.

④ Extension manifest a plugin is a row

An installable bundle of the first three. It runs nowhere near our process — it declares URLs and scopes, which is what makes third-party code safe to allow.

{
  "name": "acme-3pl", "version": "1.2.0",
  "provides": [
    {"slot":"rates","impl":"http","url":"…/rates"}
  ],
  "actions": [
    {"id":"acme.book_pickup","url":"…/pickup",
     "schema":{"order_id":"int"}}
  ],
  "emits": ["acme.pickup_booked"],
  "scopes": ["orders:read","orders:write"],
  "settings_url": "…/configure"   // iframe in admin
}

Scopes reuse the existing API-key scope system — an extension is just a key with a manifest attached, so there is no second permission model to secure.

The escape-hatch ladder — the answer to "can it do X?" is never no

Each rung costs more than the one above. A healthy product pushes customers as far up as possible, and the ladder is what lets a salesperson answer any integration question honestly.

RungWho does itExample ask

Build order — modules, tables, endpoints

Dependency-ordered. Each step is independently shippable and leaves the system working, so this can be built across weeks without a long-lived branch. New package src/platform/ — deliberately neither erp/ nor storefront/, because both sit on top of it.

#ModuleTablesWhat it does

Invariants to hold from step 1

· emit() never blocks a request — queue, then dispatch off-thread
· every event carries an idempotency key; every action is safe to run twice
· an event with no subscribers is still logged
· rule runs record input, output and status, and are replayable
· conditions are data — never eval, never exec
· a failing extension degrades its slot, never the request

What already exists to build on

More than it looks. fire_webhooks() is an event dispatcher with HMAC signing. Scoped, hashed API keys with a need(scope) dependency are the extension permission model. The catalog adapter is a working slot. The audit log is the run log's twin. Roughly a third of this is refactoring what's there rather than new code.

Order of business value

Steps 1–4 give the whole automation story and are the demo that sells it. Step 2 alone unlocks every "we already use X" objection. Steps 6 and 8 are what turn this from an internal capability into a platform other people build on — do them only once a partner actually asks, or you'll design the manifest against an imaginary user.

Reference · the P&L walk

From revenue to EBITDA

The ladder every operator and investor reads top to bottom. Each rung subtracts one class of cost — the discipline is knowing which line a cost belongs on.

Gross merchandise value (GMV) everything customers put in carts100%
− discounts, refunds, returns→ Net revenue · the only "revenue" that counts
− COGS product, packaging, inbound freight→ Gross profit
− variable selling costs shipping, processing fees, affiliate commission→ Contribution margin
− marketing & sales ads, promos, sales labour→ Contribution after marketing
− SG&A / opex salaries, rent, software, insurance→ Operating income (EBIT)
+ D&A depreciation & amortisation — non-cash= EBITDA
− interest − taxes (and D&A back out)→ Net income
Why EBITDA

It strips out financing choices (interest), jurisdiction (taxes) and accounting for past capital spend (D&A) — so two businesses can be compared on operating performance. It's the usual basis for valuation multiples.

EBITA vs EBITDA

EBITA adds back only amortisation, leaving depreciation as a cost — a fairer read for asset-heavy operations (trucks, kitchen equipment) where wear is a real expense. EBITDA is the common quote; EBITA is the honest one when you own a fleet.

Where it misleads

EBITDA ignores working capital and capex — a brand can post healthy EBITDA while cash drains into inventory. Read it beside cash conversion cycle and free cash flow, never alone.

The headline rates

CVR (conversion rate) = orders ÷ sessions — the whole funnel in one number.
RPV (revenue per visitor) = net revenue ÷ sessions — CVR × AOV, and the better target because it can't be gamed by discounting.
Micro vs macro conversion — an email signup is a micro conversion; a purchase is the macro one.

Attribution words

Attribution window — how long a click keeps credit (ours: 30 days for affiliates).
Last-click vs first-click — which touch gets the sale.
Blended CAC / MER — total spend ÷ all new customers, ignoring platform-reported ROAS, which double-counts.
Incrementality — sales that wouldn't have happened without the ad.

Where the leverage is

Funnel gains compound multiplicatively: +10% ATC and +10% checkout completion is +21% orders on the same traffic — usually cheaper than buying 21% more traffic. That's what the A/B engine, abandoned-cart playbook and drop-off detection are pointed at.
